SSAMO — Shady Side Academy Mathematics Olympiad

Olympiad · founder
April 2026
5 schools · 25 problems

The first-ever olympiad Ryan Gong and I founded and ran at our school. Months of original problem design, school-board funding, logistics — the kind of project that, two years ago, I would've started with a college-app angle in mind. I didn't this time. I just wanted the contest to exist.

We started writing problems in October. Argued about each one for elegance, for difficulty, for whether the answer was findable or actually interesting. By April we had 25 problems we were proud of, a venue at our school, registrations from multiple schools, and a board-approved budget that took two meetings and a written proposal to get.

The thing nobody tells you about writing an olympiad: you watch your problems get solved from the other side of the table. You see which problems people get stuck on. You see which ones get cracked in fifteen minutes by a kid you've never met. You see the exact configuration on Problem 4 that you spent two weeks polishing land cleanly — or, occasionally, not land at all, because the freshman in row three saw it three different ways and picked the one we hadn't thought of.

Some of them solved problems we'd been proud of writing. Some got stuck on problems we thought would be easy. It was the first time I'd ever watched my own math from the other side of the table. — from "Chasing dreams a long way back"

ARC — AIME Roadmap & Curriculum

Textbook · w/ Ryan Gong
Combi done · ~600 pp
A · NT · C · G

Free olympiad-prep textbook for kids who can't access expensive prep, in a moment when online resources have been compromised by cheating scandals. Combinatorics is done — around 600 pages of it. The other three sections are in progress; we're aiming to drop the full thing before September so kids have it for AMC season.

People have asked me, more or less directly: who are you to write a textbook? You barely made USAMO. You scored a 5.

The honest answer is that they probably shouldn't read it if what they want is to learn from the best. There are plenty of resources by MOP kids and IMO medalists — Evan Chen's notes alone are probably better than anything I'll write in my lifetime. If you're a top-ten student in the country, ARC is not for you.

ARC is for the kid scoring 75 on the AMC 10 who doesn't know if they'll ever break 100. For the kid who just made AIME and has no idea what to study next. For someone who has the passion but doesn't have the resources to make the next jump. That was me, once. — why we're writing it

The reason I think I can write something useful for that kid isn't that I'm the best at math. It's that I remember exactly what it felt like to not understand, in a way that someone who's been good at it since they were eight doesn't. Every technique in the book is a technique I personally got wrong before I got it right, and I remember the wrong versions.

Random Schrödinger Operators & Anderson Localization

w/ Mei, Shaw, Tang advised by Prof. Wencai Liu \(\gamma \approx 0.019\) Bernoulli · \(0.008\) uniform

Studying eigenvector decay in discrete random Schrödinger operators on the lattice, with i.i.d. potentials on the diagonal of a tridiagonal matrix. We computed the asymptotic decay rate \(\gamma\) for three distributions — Bernoulli \(\{0,1\}\), discrete uniform with parameter \(p\), and continuous uniform on \([0,1]\) — at \(N = 500\), \(K = 10{,}000\) Monte Carlo. The variance-to-rate map is not a simple proportionality.

On the right: a \(7\times 7\) slice of the tridiagonal matrix on top, with the diagonal flickering through Bernoulli or uniform values. The decay curve \(\mathbb{E}(Q(k))\) plots itself, log-scale. Each flicker is a fresh Monte Carlo sample — the dot on the curve is where that realization's \(\gamma\) lands. Watch it bounce around the asymptotic value. Toggle the distribution to see \(\gamma\) change.

A Sharp Piecewise Generalization of Nesbitt's Inequality

preprint · 2026 \(\alpha \in (0,1]\) phase at \(\log_{2}(3/2)\)

Establishes a sharp lower bound for the cyclic power sum \(\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\!\left(\frac{a}{b+c}\right)^{\!\alpha}\) on positive reals for \(\alpha \in (0,1]\), with a phase transition at \(\alpha^{*} = \log_{2}(3/2) \approx 0.585\). Above the threshold, the symmetric minimum \(3\cdot 2^{-\alpha}\) is sharp; below it, the infimum is \(2\), approached as one variable vanishes. Recovers classical Nesbitt at \(\alpha=1\).

Slide \(\alpha\) below to see the phase transition. At \(\alpha = 1\) you get Nesbitt's classical \(3/2\). Below \(\alpha \approx 0.585\) the boundary configuration wins. The kink in the curve is the result.

Multivariable Calculus, written for peers

360 pages iPe + LaTeX · classroom reference

Started sophomore year when our BC Calc teacher was out for three weeks and three substitutes gave up trying to teach the class. I ended up running the room for most of those three weeks — and afterwards, wrote a 360-page Calc 3 textbook so my classmates would have something they could actually read. Vector fields, line integrals, 3D solids all drawn in iPe. Used by this year's Calc 3 cohort.

On the right: a vector field. \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (-y,\, x)\), the classic rotational. Particles drift along the field lines because that's what particles do in a rotational field. The book's first chapter starts with this exact picture.
