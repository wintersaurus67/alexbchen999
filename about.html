From sprinter to architect.

Math, swimming, piano, and a long-running argument with myself about what counts as time well spent. The first three are clear. The argument is ongoing.

01
The architect /
sprinter.
An essay.

For most of middle school I treated math like a sprint — find the clever trick, get the answer, move on. If a problem didn't reveal itself in ten minutes, I felt the urge to abandon it. I was good enough at the sprint to keep getting away with it, which was the problem. Every shortcut I got away with was a shortcut I kept reaching for.

Almost a decade of competitive swimming has taught me that quitting in the middle of a hard set guarantees you'll never improve. You don't get a personal best by staring at the scoreboard. You get it by obsessing over the timing of your flipturns and the entry angle of your arm — for two and a half hours at a time, before the sun is up, with no audience.

The black line at the bottom of a pool and a stubborn proof are the same object.

Eventually I realized that the black line and a difficult proof are the same thing. They both require you to exist in a state of discomfort for a long time without a guaranteed reward. Learning to negotiate with that exhaustion — learning to do something even when you're in pain, learning to be consistent and disciplined and not just motivated — is where I first learned to be a student.

So I started sitting with problems longer. I let confusion be part of the process. Over time the sprinter turned into something else: an architect looking for structural integrity rather than a clever shortcut. I wrote a 360-page multivariable calculus textbook for my peers. I founded SSAMO, the first Shady Side Academy Mathematics Olympiad, to make olympiad math accessible to kids who never thought it was for them. I started research in mathematical physics with Prof. Wencai Liu at Texas A&M.

The thread isn't the math. It's the willingness to stay with something difficult past the point where the trick stops working. That's the same skill at 5:15 AM on a pool deck, at midnight inside a Chopin ballade, and on a black diamond at Whistler in March.

When you stop focusing on yourself, you improve more than if you lock yourself in a room and grind.

The deepest lesson of junior year wasn't a proof technique. It was that all of the work I'd assumed was a distraction from getting good — running Math Club, designing problems for other students, writing the textbook Ryan Gong and I co-authored — turned out to be the thing that made me better. When you have to explain why something works to a kid who's never seen it before, you find out fast which parts of your understanding are real and which are just pattern-matching.

I scored a 5 on the USAMO. The median was 6. For about a week I treated this like a verdict on six years of my life. Then I noticed I was happier when I stopped checking my email every twenty minutes for the cutoff release, and I went back to math, and the version of math I went back to was the version where other people were also in the room. That's the version that's stuck.

I'm heading to the Ross Mathematics Program for summer 2026 — six weeks doing nothing but number theory, the subject I avoided for years and now want to face. After that, senior year. And after that, an applied-math PhD I haven't quite earned the right to dream about yet, but I'm working on it.
