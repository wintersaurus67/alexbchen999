§ 01 · About

Five things. They all bleed into each other more than you'd think.

The thing I keep finding is that nothing I do is its own thing. The card tricks turned into a math obsession. The math obsession turned out to require the same kind of stubbornness I'd learned in the pool. The pool turned out to require the same focus I'd built up at the piano. The piano taught me what discipline is for, which is part of why skiing — the one place I get to switch all of that off — matters so much.

Below: magic, math, swim, piano, ski. In that order, because that's roughly how I picked them up, and roughly how they connect to each other.

I have the hands of a polite raccoon.

During COVID I tried to learn magic. The first trick I tried was an ace trick — triple lift, then double, then single, aces materialize. I practiced for weeks, filmed myself, and discovered the double lift was visible from space. The triple was a war crime. I gave up on sleight of hand pretty quickly after that.

What I switched to is way more fun anyway: self-working tricks, where the math does the work and your hands just have to deal cards in the right order. Below is one. Twelve face-down cards, six and six. Pick aces from the tray on the right, then click a gap between cards to slide them in.

I performed one of these once for a kid at a Chinese-school event. Maybe nine years old. He watched it three times in a row, frowned, then said "wait, are you just doing math?" I told him yes. He looked betrayed for about two seconds, asked me to teach him, and we spent the next forty minutes figuring out together why a stack of 16 cards has to behave this way.

That's the thing about self-working tricks. The audience eventually asks why does this work, and at that point you're not doing magic anymore. You're doing math with cards as props. The kids who keep asking — those are the ones I want to write a textbook for.

So if you keep asking why, you end up here.

Self-working tricks are math problems where the props happen to be cards. Once you're hooked on why does this work, you eventually stop needing the cards — you stop wanting to perform, you just want the next problem. Competition math was the next obvious step.

For my USAMTS submission this year I got a geometry problem I really liked — Problem 4 from Round 3. The kind that looks intimidating, has a million points and lines on the diagram, then collapses once you spot the right structure. A triangle has so many natural "centers" (circumcenter, orthocenter, centroid) that you can cascade them into nested triangles. The nested triangles end up homothetic to the original, and the homothety centers all line up on the Euler line. Concurrency falls out.

Here's something I figured out late: the part of competition math that gets you good at competition math isn't the clever insight. The clever insight is the part that gets you the points once you have it. The part that's actually load-bearing is the willingness to sit with one problem for a few hours without confirmation that the problem has a solution at all.

I learned that in the pool, weirdly. Long before I learned it on a math problem.

The black line and a stubborn proof are the same object.

I've been swimming competitively for almost a decade. Right now I'm at roughly 16 hours a week, which means somewhere around 832 hours a year, which is roughly one entire month of waking life spent staring at a black line at the bottom of a pool.

Almost everything I know about not quitting I learned in the pool. There's a specific kind of exhaustion that hits you about 1500 yards into a hard practice — your body has given you all the information it can possibly give you about whether continuing is sensible (it isn't), and you keep going anyway, because that's the deal. The exact same skill applies to a USAMO problem at hour three when nothing's working and you're starting to question whether the problem is even solvable. The skill is: don't quit yet.

People ask what I think about for two and a half hours. Honest answer: usually nothing, occasionally everything, sometimes I'm running probability calculations from last night's AIME problem in my head, and once during a brutal 200 IM set I genuinely forgot the lyrics to a song I'd been humming and got so frustrated I gave up and thought about Nesbitt's inequality for the rest of practice. I don't recommend it as a coping strategy.

Best swim of my life so far: a 22.2 split on the 50 free at WPIAL States this year, dropping 1.8 seconds to close out our relay. Probably the most emotional I've been at a meet — possibly tied with watching a teammate qualify for States off a split I brought home, which is its own thing.

The one place the discipline trick doesn't work.

Here's the part that surprised me. The "stay with it until it cracks" thing transferred from swim to math really cleanly. I assumed it would also transfer to piano. It hasn't, or at least not in the way I expected.

Ten years of piano. I can play, technically, almost anything in the standard repertoire. Chopin ballades, Liszt études, Rachmaninoff preludes. I have the fingers, the rhythm, the muscle memory. Carmel Klavier Grand Prize in 2019, second prize in 2021. A few Pittsburgh International firsts, a couple of Grand Prix, the Steinway Society of Western PA audition. I'm not, however, a good pianist.

There's a difference between playing the notes and playing the piece. The difference is the part where the listener feels something, and that part — the musicality — is something I've never quite cracked. I am, by my own honest assessment, a robot who has read a manual on how to feel things. I produce the correct outputs. The outputs do not move people. They move me slightly when I'm alone, but I think this is the piano equivalent of laughing at your own joke.

The lesson, I think, is that some things don't get better just because you put in the hours. Swim gets better with hours. Math gets better with hours. Piano needs something else, and I don't quite have it yet. Maybe at twenty-five. Maybe never. I'm OK with not knowing yet.

The one place I'm allowed to turn it off.

OK so: swim teaches discipline. Math uses that same discipline. Piano teaches you what discipline can't reach. And then, twice a year, I get to go to the one place where I switch all of it off.

Christmas in Colorado — usually Vail, Breckenridge, or Keystone. Spring break in Whistler. Whistler is the big one.

For context: most of my life is a tightly controlled exercise in not making stupid choices. Don't skip practice. Don't blow off a homework set. Don't take the dumb risk on a USAMO problem when the safer approach gets you the points. The whole architecture of being a serious student is built around the systematic suppression of recklessness, and most days I'm fine with that.

Then I get to Whistler and I ski Purple Haze, which is a chute that drops at something like 40 degrees and ends in a cliff band, and I think finally. Spanky's Ladder, DOA, the whole back side. I'm not Olympic. I'm not even close. But the runs people warn you about are the ones I look forward to, and the version of me on a black diamond in March is somehow the most me version of me there is.

I think it works because skiing is the one thing in my life where the answer to "is this a good idea" is who cares, let's find out. You don't get to ask that question much when you're trying to qualify for USAMO. You don't get to ask it on the blocks for the 100 free. But at the top of a chute, with nobody to impress and nothing on the line except the next two minutes, the question stops being a question. You just go.

usamts 37 · round 3 · problem 4

Four lines, one point.

$O$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle ABC$, $H$ the orthocenter. $O_A$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle BOC$, define $O_B, O_C$ similarly. $H_A$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle OO_BO_C$, similarly $H_B, H_C$. Prove $AH_A$, $BH_B$, $CH_C$, and $HO$ are concurrent.

  1. Start with $\triangle ABC$, its circumcircle, $O$ (circumcenter), and $H$ (orthocenter). Standard setup.
  2. Build $O_A$, $O_B$, $O_C$ — each is the circumcenter of a small triangle with $O$ and two vertices. The line $OO_A$ is exactly the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ (similar for the others).
  3. Now the same construction one level up: $H_A$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle OO_BO_C$, similarly $H_B$, $H_C$. The new triangle $H_AH_BH_C$ comes out parallel to $ABC$ — the sides are radical axes.
  4. Look at the midpoint triangle $M_AM_BM_C$. It's homothetic to $ABC$ at the centroid $G$ (factor 2), and homothetic to $H_AH_BH_C$ at $O$.
  5. Parallel triangles ⇒ homothetic. So $AH_A, BH_B, CH_C$ concur at the homothety center. Call it $P$.
  6. Composition of homotheties: $T_3 = T_2 \circ T_1$ has its center on the line through the two original centers. $P$ lies on $OG$, and Euler says $O, G, H$ are collinear, so $P$ lies on $OH$. Done. $\blacksquare$
