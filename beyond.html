The set is honest. The 3:00 free at the end of each round is where it stops being a workout.

Nine years competitive. Roughly sixteen hours a week in season, which works out to one entire month of waking life per year spent staring at a black line. Short course, because my turns are the only thing I really have over the long-course guys, and long course just feels too long.

WPIAL States qualifier in the 50 and 100 free. Best swim of my life so far is the 22.2 split I dropped to close out our relay last year — 1.8 seconds off my previous best, on a cold morning in February, in a meet I'd been losing sleep over for two weeks. I won't pretend the time matters to anyone but me. It does to me.

My coach said something to me once that I'm paraphrasing because I don't remember it word-for-word — you can feel like absolute shit in the water and still get something out of the set. I think about that one a lot. Not just in the pool.

The thing the pool has taught me is that the feeling of this is pointless, I'm done is almost never a reliable signal. Sometimes it's information about the workout. Sometimes it's information about how I slept. Sometimes it's just noise that goes away in 200 yards. The skill isn't pushing through every time, it's figuring out which one of those three it is without quitting first.

Some of my best math happens in the half-hour after practice — exhausted, walking home, brain too tired to talk itself out of an idea. — what I tell people who ask why I bother with a 5:15 alarm

I don't actually know whether the swim makes the math better or whether the kind of person willing to do both is just a kind of person. I suspect it's the second one and I tell myself it's the first one, because telling yourself the second one starts to feel like cheating.

Beethoven's Pathétique, all three movements. The same notes, twice. You'll hear the difference.

Ten years of piano. Chopin Ballade No. 2. Liszt's La Campanella. Rachmaninoff Études-tableaux. The fingers are there. The musicality, by my teacher's own honest assessment, is not — very talented, gifted technique, but you can't quite express yourself yet. He's not wrong, and the part I respect about him is that he didn't soften it.

The piece that exposed it most is the Pathétique. The notes are easy. The silence between them is where the music is, and I play the silences like a metronome ticks them. Two versions of the same piece. The robot is what I sound like. The rubato is what the music actually wants.

I am, by my own honest assessment, a robot who has read a manual on how to feel things. I produce the correct outputs. The outputs do not move people. They move me slightly when I'm alone, but I think this is the piano equivalent of laughing at your own joke.

Funniest part: my favorite composer is Chopin, and Chopin is the one I'm objectively worst at. The whole point of Chopin is rubato — the small expressive stretches and contractions of time, the breathing. Everything I have works against that. I keep playing him anyway. The Ballade No. 2 in F major is my current war. I will lose it again, slowly, over the next year.

I performed last in the Heinz Hall lobby — Pittsburgh Symphony's main hall, but the lobby, which is the right amount of "this matters" and "nobody's actually here for you." Recordings of most of this are on my YouTube channel. Proceed with appropriate expectations.

The lesson, I think, is that some things don't get better just because you put in the hours. Swim gets better with hours. Math gets better with hours. Piano needs something else, and I don't have it yet. — what I've ended up believing, ten years in

Maybe at twenty-five. Maybe never. I'm OK with not knowing yet.

What I'm good at.

If the Pathétique is the piece that exposed me, the Liszt-Paganini etudes are the ones that don't. They're not about anything. They're studies — Liszt transcribed Paganini's violin caprices for piano and made them harder than the originals. Tremolo. Octaves. La Campanella. Arpeggios that cover the whole keyboard in one bar. A hunt. A theme and twelve variations that ends with both hands doing things hands aren't supposed to do simultaneously.

None of this needs me to feel anything. It needs me to land the notes. That I can do. All six below, in order.

Most of my life is a tightly-controlled exercise in not making stupid choices. Don't skip practice. Don't blow off the problem set. Don't take the dumb risk on the USAMO problem when the safer approach gets you the points. The whole architecture of being a serious student is built around the systematic suppression of recklessness, and most days I'm fine with that.

Then I get to Whistler and the answer to is this a good idea? stops being a question. Tree skiing. Moguls. Anything with air. The version of me looking down a chute in March is somehow the most me version of me there is.

iv.
double lift.

eventually fixed.

The polite raccoon, the war crime, and the trick I came back to.

The first trick I ever tried — COVID year, thirteen years old — was an ace production. Triple lift, then double, then single, the aces appear from the top of the deck. The trick depends entirely on the lift looking like one card. Mine didn't. The double was visible from space. The triple was a war crime. I filmed myself doing it, watched it back, and concluded that my hands had the dexterity of a polite raccoon.

So I switched to self-working — tricks where modular arithmetic and permutation parity do the work and your hands just deal the cards in the right order. The Gilbreath Principle is the cleanest one. Alternate the deck red-black. Let the spectator riffle shuffle however they want. Deal in pairs. Every pair is one red and one black, every time. Not marked cards, not sleight, just a fact about how a riffle interacts with an alternating sequence. There's a working version of it on the Reach page.

self-working · Gilbreathidle

The funny part is I eventually went back. The hands actually did improve — not at thirteen, not for years, but I came back to the double lift sometime in eleventh grade and could finally make it look like one card. Then the pinky break, then the side steal, then a center-deal that's still ugly but no longer a war crime. The first trick I tried and gave up on is now the trick I open with.

I don't perform much. I do tricks for friends in the math room when we're stuck on a problem and need a break, for kids at Chinese-school events who watch a self-working trick three times in a row before asking wait, are you just doing math?, and occasionally, badly, at parties. The polite-raccoon line was true at thirteen and I get to be a little wrong about it now.

The audience eventually asks why does this work. At that point you're not doing magic anymore. You're doing math with cards as props.— the thing I keep coming back to
