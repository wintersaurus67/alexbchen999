i · geometry · usamo 2021 / p1

Three circumcircles, one point.

Rectangles $BCC_1B_2$, $CAA_1C_2$, $ABB_1A_2$ are erected outside an acute $\triangle ABC$, with a certain angle condition I won't write out. Prove that the lines $A_1B_2$, $B_1C_2$, $C_1A_2$ are concurrent.

proof · six lines step 1 / 6
  1. The triangle and three rectangles, erected outward. Each rectangle has a circumscribed circle — its diagonals are equal and bisect each other, so a circle passes through all four corners.
  2. Call the three circumcircles $\omega_A$ (through $C, A, A_1, C_2$), $\omega_B$ (through $A, B, B_1, A_2$), $\omega_C$ (through $B, C, C_1, B_2$).
  3. The angle condition forces the three circumcircles to concur at one point. Call it $P$. This is the whole problem. The rest is one line.
  4. $CB_2$ is a diagonal of rectangle $BCC_1B_2$, so it's a diameter of $\omega_C$. Thales: $\angle CPB_2 = 90^\circ$.
  5. Symmetrically, $CA_1$ is a diameter of $\omega_A$, so $\angle CPA_1 = 90^\circ$.
  6. Two right angles at $P$ on $\overline{CP}$, opposite sides, force $A_1, P, B_2$ collinear. So $P$ lies on $A_1B_2$. Same argument for $B_1C_2$ and $C_1A_2$. The three lines all pass through $P$. $\blacksquare$
the whole problem is finding $P$. P

How I got to the page.

The whole problem is finding the point.

I like this proof a lot. Six lines, five of them obvious; the whole difficulty is locating $P$, after which it's basically trivial. I've thought about it more than I probably should have.

Looking back, my actual math life has worked roughly like this. There were maybe one or two moments that, at the time, looked like nothing. They were everything.

[middle school]

I took the AMC 8 for the first time in 6th grade and scored a $12/25$. I genuinely thought "stars and bars" was a phrase about flags. I looked through the AoPS wiki afterward and didn't understand half the solutions.

7th grade I scored an $18/25$, one point off honor roll. I was annoyed about that one point for longer than I should've been.

8th grade my flight got cancelled the night before and I had about two hours of sleep. I still scored a $19/25$ — made honor roll by two points — and spent the next week telling everyone I would've scored higher if I'd slept (true, but obnoxious). That was probably the first time I really cared about a math score.

It was also the year a teacher pulled me aside and told me — gently — that I should stop dreaming about AIME. That my goals weren't realistic. Focus on academics. Be a normal kid.

I qualified the next year. We never talked about it.

[freshman year]

BC Calculus with juniors and seniors. Almost everyone in the room was three years older than me. $115.5$ on 10A (top 5%), $5$ on the AIME I. Neither of those is what I actually remember from that year.

Our BC Calc teacher was out for three weeks. Three different substitutes tried to run the room and gave up. I ended up teaching most of those weeks — going through problems on the board, taking questions, even assigning homework. I was 14. The kids in front of me were 17 and 18. Some of them are still my closest friends.

I didn't process it at the time, but those three weeks were probably worth more than any score I got that year. It was the first time I'd taught math instead of just done it. The two things turn out to be very different.

[sophomore year]

I wrote a 360-page multivariable calculus textbook for my classmates. I took Physics C and got cooked by magnetism. I was also, in retrospect, kind of insufferable — my parents had this thing where they'd yell "HOW CAN YOU NOT UNDERSTAND THIS" at me whenever I got problems wrong, and a chunk of that, I'm sorry to report, leaked into how I treated people who asked me questions.

I dropped to a $94.5$ on the 12B. Then somehow scored an $8$ on the AIME with basically no prep. Someone told me an index of $192.5$ might sneak into USAJMO. Mine was exactly $192.5$. I got excited.

The actual cutoff came back at $242.5$. Fifty points short. I didn't do math for two weeks.

Then that spring my best friend Ryan Gong joined the school. We met on the soccer pitch of all places. We didn't really do math together until the end of the year, and at the time it didn't feel like anything in particular.

Anyway. More on that later.

A short tangent.
On infinite descent and why
I needed it sophomore year.

On descent.

A lot of my favorite proofs work by going down. Assume the thing is true. Build a smaller version. Build a smaller version of that. The whole proof rests on the descent eventually having nowhere to go.

The positive integers have a floor — you can't get smaller than $1$. So if your problem forces an infinite chain of strictly smaller positive integers, the chain is impossible and the assumption was wrong. This is infinite descent. People have been doing it since Fermat.

Sophomore year I kept feeling like I was in one of these chains. $115.5$ to $94.5$. The $192.5$ that should have been enough and wasn't. I think I half-believed there was no floor at all, that the descent just kept going. The thing that Vieta jumping makes formal is: if it feels unbounded, you're probably wrong about something. Either you stop, or the setup was lying to you.

Sophomore-year me would've benefited from hearing that.

ii · number theory · imo 1988 / p6

Vieta jumping, infinite descent.

If $a, b$ are positive integers and $ab + 1 \mid a^2 + b^2$, then $\frac{a^2 + b^2}{ab + 1}$ is a perfect square.

proof · by descent step 1 / 6
  1. Suppose, for contradiction, $c = \frac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}$ is a positive integer but not a square. Among all valid pairs $(a, b)$ giving this $c$, choose one minimizing $a + b$, with $a \geq b$ WLOG.
  2. Fixing $b$ and $c$, expand the equation as $P(x) = x^2 - bc \cdot x + (b^2 - c) = 0$, with $a$ as one root.
  3. By Vieta's, the second root $r_2$ satisfies $\textbf{(1)}\; r_2 = bc - a$ (so $r_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$) and $\textbf{(2)}\; r_2 = \frac{b^2 - c}{a}$ (so $r_2 \ne 0$, since $c$ is not a square).
  4. Plugging $r_2$ in: $c = \frac{r_2^2 + b^2}{r_2 b + 1}$. Since $c > 0$ and $b > 0$, the denominator $r_2 b + 1 > 0$, forcing $r_2 > 0$.
  5. From $\textbf{(2)}$: $r_2 = \frac{b^2 - c}{a} < \frac{b^2}{a} \leq a$ (using $a \geq b$ and $c > 0$). So $r_2 + b < a + b$.
  6. But $(r_2, b)$ is also a valid pair giving the same $c$, with $r_2 + b < a + b$ — contradicting the minimality of $a + b$. So $c$ must have been a square after all. $\blacksquare$
descent into $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ — which is well-ordered. ⤓
$(a, b)$ sum = a + b
⤓ vieta-jump
$(r_2, b)$ < a + b
⤓ vieta-jump
$(r_2', b)$ smaller still
⤓ ⋯
$\cdots$ infinite descent
Each pair has a strictly smaller positive sum. But $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ is well-ordered — descent must terminate. Contradiction.

After the jump.

That summer between sophomore and junior year, Ryan and I started solving problems together and basically didn't stop. He's a number theory main; I'm combinatorics and geometry. We grinded past USAMTS, USAMO, IMO, CMO (Chinese and Canadian), TST, TSTST.

Before Ryan, "studying" meant doing past problems and checking my answers. After Ryan, "studying" meant sitting at a table for three hours arguing about whether a particular induction setup actually worked. We almost never wrote anything down. The point was the conversation. I look back at that summer and most of what I remember is laughing, then being stuck, then laughing some more.

[junior year]

I became president of our Math Club, with Ryan as VP. The previous seniors had all graduated and nobody really felt like part of anything. Ryan and I went to the administration and asked for actual funding — registration fees for outside competitions, travel costs for ARML and Nationals, materials. We had to write a proposal, talk to a lot of people. We got it.

Two meetings a week. A competition track and a learning track. A roster of who was going where. We took the team to BrUMO at Brown and PuMAC at Princeton. We started designing our own problems for mocks instead of pulling from past contests.

Sometime in the fall Ryan and I had this idea: what if we hosted our own olympiad? Not a mock. An actual one, with real registrations, students from other schools, problems we wrote ourselves. We pitched it. We got a venue. We wrote the problems over months — arguing about each one for elegance, for difficulty, for whether the solution was actually interesting and not just findable.

In April we ran SSAMO — the first-ever Shady Side Academy Mathematics Olympiad. Kids from multiple schools showed up. Some of them solved problems we'd been proud of. Some got stuck on problems we thought were easy. It was the first time I'd watched my own math from the other side of the table. It was kind of amazing.

When you stop focusing on yourself, you actually improve more than if you lock yourself in a room and grind.

[the 12, the 74, the 5]

December: $117 / 127.5$ on AMC 12A/B. With the cheating-scandal cutoffs that year I'd need a $13$ or $14$ on the AIME to qualify through the AMC route. I'd never broken $8$.

So I made a decision that sounded, at the time, kind of insane: I stopped studying for the AIME. Not entirely — I'd already mocked basically every past one. But for the six weeks before the test, I spent the time working through the application problem sets for SUMaC, PROMYS, and Ross. Long, formal, written-up-in-LaTeX problems that rewarded sitting with one thing for a week. The opposite of timed prep. My friends called this strategy a "空城计". I told myself it was probably going to be a disaster.

I scored a $12$. Biggest single-year jump I've ever made. I didn't realize until later that the camp apps had been doing AIME prep all along, just for a different skill — the skill that rewards sitting with a problem long enough to actually understand it, which is the part most kids skip.

Cutoffs were brutal that year. I waited for the USAMO invite. It didn't come through the AMC route. A few days later it came through USAMTS — I'd scored a $74$ (25 / 24 / 25) in the fall and that was enough to get me in through the talent-search route. My testing site was Vancouver, because I was skiing at Whistler that week.

Day 1 I thought I solved P1 cleanly and made decent progress on P2. Day 2 I had a clean induction on P4 and recognized the Miquel-point configuration on P5. I walked out thinking I had maybe a $21$.

I got a $5$. Median was $6$.

I was mad for about a week. Then something weirder happened — I noticed I was happier. The constant background pressure of am I going to make USAMO had been silenced for the first time in years, and I hadn't realized how loud it had been until it stopped.

What I came back to wasn't past USAMO problems. It was the team. Freshmen trying to qualify for AIME for the first time, asking me what to study. The ARC textbook Ryan and I had started — a free olympiad-prep book for kids without expensive resources, four sections (Algebra, Number Theory, Combinatorics, Geometry). Combinatorics is around 600 pages and basically done. We're aiming to release before AMC season.

I guess the descent did stop. Not because I'd hit any specific floor — I hadn't — but because I'd stopped checking how far down I was. Which isn't really what Vieta jumping predicts, but whatever, the analogy was never going to be perfect.

On research.

Olympiad math is closer to real math than school math is, but it isn't real math. Olympiad problems are designed by humans who already solved them — somewhere in someone's notebook the answer is written down. The path is hard to find, but the path exists.

Research isn't like this. Nobody knows if there's an answer. There's no answer key, no coordinator, just the question and you and the live possibility that the thing you're trying to prove is false, or unprovable, or true but inaccessible to the tools you have. You can spend a year on something and get nothing.

With olympiad problems, when I'm stuck, the stuck feeling is "I'm not seeing the trick." With research, the stuck feeling is "the trick might not exist." Those are pretty different feelings. One is solvable by trying harder.

I haven't done much yet, but I want to do a lot. I worked with PReMA over the past year on a paper about random Schrödinger operators and Anderson localization — supervised by Prof. Wencai Liu at Texas A&M. Still in the pipeline. Then there's the Nesbitt thing (next section). I noticed it during English class one day and couldn't stop thinking about it. Most of it got worked out the same afternoon.

Heading to Ross this summer for six weeks of number theory — the subject I avoided for years, which is why I want to do it. I got into SUMaC and PROMYS as well, and spent two whole weeks staring at the three acceptance letters before picking Ross. I think it reflects who I am better than the other two would.

Next year: senior year, ARC release before AMC season, another SSAMO, and another USAMO attempt. This time wanting to medal. I know how the contradiction sounds — the score isn't the point, also I want a specific score — but both things are true at the same time. I think they have to be.

A sharp piecewise generalization of Nesbitt.

Last one. For positive reals $a, b, c$ and an exponent $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, the cyclic sum $\sum_{\text{cyc}}\left(\frac{a}{b+c}\right)^{\alpha}$ has a sharp lower bound, with a phase transition at $\alpha^\star = \log_2(3/2)$. Like I said in the last interlude, I noticed it in English class.

proof sketch · six lines step 1 / 6
  1. Classical Nesbitt: $\displaystyle\sum_{\text{cyc}} \frac{a}{b+c} \geq \frac{3}{2}$, with equality at $a = b = c$.
  2. Raise each term to a power $\alpha \in (0, 1]$. Study $f_\alpha(a, b, c) = \sum_{\text{cyc}} \left(\frac{a}{b+c}\right)^{\!\alpha}$.
  3. On the symmetric ray $a = b = c$, each fraction equals $1/2$, so $f_\alpha = 3 \cdot 2^{-\alpha} = 3/2^{\alpha}$.
  4. On the degenerate boundary $c \to 0^+$, $a = b$: two fractions tend to $1^\alpha = 1$, the third to $0$. Sum $\to 2$.
  5. Compare: $3/2^\alpha = 2 \iff \alpha = \log_2(3/2) \approx 0.585$. Below this, the boundary wins; above, the symmetric point wins.
  6. Sharp lower bound: $f_\alpha \geq \min\!\left(2,\; \frac{3}{2^{\alpha}}\right)$, attained at one of the two extremal configurations. $\blacksquare$
writing this up as a preprint — phase transition at $\alpha^\star$. α*
$\left(\dfrac{a}{b+c}\right)^{\alpha}$ + $\left(\dfrac{b}{c+a}\right)^{\alpha}$ + $\left(\dfrac{c}{a+b}\right)^{\alpha}$
$\geq$ $\min\!\left(2,\; \dfrac{3}{2^{\alpha}}\right)$
α = 0
α* ≈ .585
α = 1
