Three circumcircles, one point.

Rectangles $BCC_1B_2$, $CAA_1C_2$, $ABB_1A_2$ are erected outside an acute $\triangle ABC$, satisfying the problem's angle condition. Prove that the three lines $A_1B_2$, $B_1C_2$, $C_1A_2$ are concurrent.

proof · six lines step 1 / 6
  1. The triangle and three rectangles, erected outward. Each rectangle has a circumscribed circle — its diagonals are equal in length and bisect each other, so a circle passes through all four corners.
  2. Call the three circumcircles $\omega_A$ (through $C, A, A_1, C_2$), $\omega_B$ (through $A, B, B_1, A_2$), $\omega_C$ (through $B, C, C_1, B_2$).
  3. The problem's angle condition forces the three circumcircles to concur at one point. Call it $P$. This is where all the substance of the problem lives — every line afterward is a one-line consequence.
  4. Since $CB_2$ is a diagonal of the rectangle $BCC_1B_2$, it's a diameter of $\omega_C$. Thales: $\angle CPB_2 = 90^\circ$.
  5. Symmetrically, $CA_1$ is a diameter of $\omega_A$, so $\angle CPA_1 = 90^\circ$.
  6. Two right angles at $P$ both standing on $\overline{CP}$ from opposite sides force $A_1, P, B_2$ to be collinear. So $P$ lies on $A_1B_2$. The same argument places $P$ on $B_1C_2$ and $C_1A_2$. The three lines concur at $P$. $\blacksquare$
"Once you have $P$, the rest is one line." P

Vieta jumping, infinite descent.

If $a, b$ are positive integers and $ab + 1 \mid a^2 + b^2$, then $\frac{a^2 + b^2}{ab + 1}$ is a perfect square.

proof · by descent step 1 / 6
  1. Suppose, for contradiction, $c = \frac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}$ is a positive integer but not a square. Among all valid pairs $(a, b)$ giving this $c$, choose one minimizing $a + b$, with $a \geq b$ WLOG.
  2. Fixing $b$ and $c$, expand the equation as $P(x) = x^2 - bc \cdot x + (b^2 - c) = 0$, with $a$ as one root.
  3. By Vieta's, the second root $r_2$ satisfies $\textbf{(1)}\; r_2 = bc - a$ (so $r_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$) and $\textbf{(2)}\; r_2 = \frac{b^2 - c}{a}$ (so $r_2 \ne 0$, since $c$ is not a square).
  4. Plugging $r_2$ in: $c = \frac{r_2^2 + b^2}{r_2 b + 1}$. Since $c > 0$ and $b > 0$, the denominator $r_2 b + 1 > 0$, forcing $r_2 > 0$.
  5. From $\textbf{(2)}$: $r_2 = \frac{b^2 - c}{a} < \frac{b^2}{a} \leq a$ (using $a \geq b$ and $c > 0$). So $r_2 + b < a + b$.
  6. But $(r_2, b)$ is also a valid pair giving the same $c$, with $r_2 + b < a + b$ — contradicting the minimality of $a + b$. So $c$ must have been a square after all. $\blacksquare$
descent into $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ — which is well-ordered. ⤓

Each pair has a strictly smaller positive sum. But $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ is well-ordered — descent must terminate. Contradiction.

An interlude.
How I got here.
What I still don't know.

Six years of tagging along.

I took my first competition math test in sixth grade. I scored a $12/25$ on the AMC 8, didn't understand half the questions, and thought "stars and bars" was a phrase about flags. The next year I scored $19/25$ and was one point off honor roll. The year after, my teacher pulled me aside to tell me I should stop dreaming about the AIME. I qualified the following year.

Freshman year I took BC Calculus with juniors and seniors, got a $115.5$ on the AMC 10A (top 5%) and a $5$ on the AIME I. I started teaching the class for three weeks when our regular teacher was out and three substitutes gave up — the first time I'd taught math instead of just done it. I didn't process this until much later, but I think those three weeks were more important than any score I got that year.

Sophomore year I wrote a 360-page multivariable calculus textbook for my classmates, took Physics C, dropped to a $94.5$ on the 12B, then scored an $8$ on the AIME with no real prep. I was 50 points short of the USAJMO cutoff. The cutoff would land in late spring; my AMC index was exactly $192.5$, the rumored magic number. The cutoff turned out to be $242.5$. I didn't do math for two weeks after that.

The Ryan factor

That spring my best friend Ryan Gong joined the school. He's a number-theory main; I'm a combinatorics-and-geometry main. We started solving olympiad problems together over the summer and didn't stop. We worked through past USAMTS, USAMO, IMO, CMO (Chinese and Canadian), TST and TSTST problems. The thing that changed wasn't the amount of practice — it was the conversation. Before Ryan, "studying" meant doing past problems and checking answers. After Ryan, "studying" meant sitting at a table for three hours arguing about whether a particular induction setup actually worked.

Junior year I became president of our school's Math Club, with Ryan as VP. We restructured the club: secured school-board funding for travel and registration fees, ran twice-weekly meetings, set up separate competition and learning tracks, brought kids to MathLeague qualifiers, took our team to BrUMO at Brown and PuMAC at Princeton. In April we ran SSAMO — the first-ever Shady Side Academy Mathematics Olympiad. Months of original problem design, kids from multiple schools, our problems on the table. The first time I'd watched my own math from the other side.

When you stop focusing on yourself, you improve more than if you lock yourself in a room and grind.

The 12, the 5, and the lesson

In December, I scored $117/127.5$ on AMC 12A/B. With cheating-scandal cutoffs pushing the index above $400$, I'd need to score $13$ or $14$ on the AIME to qualify for USAMO. I'd never broken $8$. So I made what sounded, at the time, like an insane decision: I stopped studying for the AIME. Instead I spent the six weeks before working through the application problem sets for SUMaC, PROMYS, and Ross — long, formal, written-up-in-LaTeX problems that rewarded sitting with one thing for a week. I scored a $12$ on the AIME. Biggest single-year jump I've ever made.

The USAMO invite came through the USAMTS route (I'd scored a $74$ over the fall). My testing site was Vancouver, because I was at Whistler that week. Day 1 I thought I solved P1 and made progress on P2. Day 2 I had what I thought was a clean induction on P4 and a Miquel-point argument on P5. I left thinking I had something like a $21$. I got a $5$. The median was $6$.

I was mad for a week. Then I noticed I was happier, because the constant background pressure of am I going to make USAMO had been silenced for the first time in years. What I came back to wasn't past USAMO problems. It was the Math Team. Helping freshmen who were trying to qualify for AIME for the first time. Opening the ARC textbook I'd been writing with Ryan — a free olympiad-prep book for kids without expensive resources, four sections (Algebra, Number Theory, Combinatorics, Geometry) — and starting another section.

What's next

Heading to the Ross Mathematics Program this summer — six weeks of number theory, the subject I avoided for years. I got into SUMaC and PROMYS too, and spent two full weeks staring at the three acceptance letters before choosing Ross. I think it reflects my values and character the best. After that: senior year, ARC textbook release before AMC season, another SSAMO, and another USAMO attempt — this time with the explicit goal of medaling. The contradiction of "the score isn't the point, also I want a specific score" is real. I think it has to be.

A sharp piecewise generalization of Nesbitt.

Saved this one for last, because it's mine. For positive reals $a, b, c$ and an exponent $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, the cyclic sum $\sum_{\text{cyc}}\left(\frac{a}{b+c}\right)^{\alpha}$ has a sharp lower bound with a phase transition at $\alpha^\star = \log_2(3/2)$.

proof sketch · six lines step 1 / 6
  1. Classical Nesbitt: $\displaystyle\sum_{\text{cyc}} \frac{a}{b+c} \geq \frac{3}{2}$, with equality at $a = b = c$.
  2. Raise each term to a power $\alpha \in (0, 1]$. Study $f_\alpha(a, b, c) = \sum_{\text{cyc}} \left(\frac{a}{b+c}\right)^{\!\alpha}$.
  3. On the symmetric ray $a = b = c$, each fraction equals $1/2$, so $f_\alpha = 3 \cdot 2^{-\alpha} = 3/2^{\alpha}$.
  4. On the degenerate boundary $c \to 0^+$, $a = b$: two fractions tend to $1^\alpha = 1$, the third to $0$. Sum $\to 2$.
  5. Compare the two regimes: $3/2^\alpha = 2 \iff \alpha = \log_2(3/2) \approx 0.585$. Below this threshold the boundary wins; above it, the symmetric point wins.
  6. Therefore the sharp lower bound is $f_\alpha \geq \min\!\left(2,\; \frac{3}{2^{\alpha}}\right)$, attained at one of the two extremal configurations. $\blacksquare$
